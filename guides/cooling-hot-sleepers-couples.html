Cooling solutions for hot sleepers, and couples who disagree on temperature

Sleeping hot is one of the most common complaints we see, and it often comes with a partner who is always cold. The good news is that there are fixes at every budget, from free habits to dual-zone bed systems. Here is what the science says and how to choose.

Why heat wrecks sleep

Your core body temperature falls as you drift off, and your body sheds heat through the skin, especially the hands and feet. In a classic lab study, warming of the hands and feet relative to the trunk (a sign that blood vessels there have widened to release heat) was the best predictor of how quickly people fell asleep[1]. Anything that blocks that heat loss works against you.

A review of the research on thermal environment and sleep found that in real-life conditions, with bedding and clothing, heat exposure increases wakefulness and reduces deep (slow-wave) and REM sleep, and humid heat makes it worse[2]. That is why a hot, stuffy bedroom or a mattress that traps heat leaves you waking more in the night.

Start with the free fixes

  • Cool the room. A bedroom around 60–67°F (about 16–19°C) is a commonly suggested range. Use a fan, open a window if the air outside is cooler, or set the thermostat to drop at bedtime.
  • Lower the humidity. Humid heat disrupts sleep more than dry heat[2]. A dehumidifier or air conditioning helps in muggy climates.
  • Take a warm shower or bath 1–2 hours before bed. It sounds backwards, but a meta-analysis found that water-based body heating at 40–42.5°C (104–108.5°F), scheduled 1–2 hours before bed for as little as 10 minutes, shortened the time to fall asleep and improved self-rated sleep quality[3]. Warming the skin helps the body dump heat afterwards.
  • Keep your feet comfortable. Warm hands and feet are linked to falling asleep faster[1], so light socks can help the partner who runs cold.
  • Watch alcohol and late meals. Alcohol disrupts the second half of the night[4], and many people feel warmer and more restless after a heavy late meal.

Bedding that breathes

  • Sheets: breathable, moisture-wicking fabrics such as lyocell (TENCEL), linen and percale cotton usually feel cooler than polyester microfibre or flannel. They are the cheapest upgrade for most hot sleepers.
  • Mattress protector: many waterproof protectors trap heat. Look for a breathable one.
  • Mattress: solid, dense memory foam tends to hold heat; hybrids with coils allow more airflow, and latex usually sleeps cooler than memory foam. The mattress quiz counts “sleeps hot” as a factor in its ideal design.
  • Weighted blankets: a randomized trial found weighted chain blankets reduced insomnia severity in adults with psychiatric disorders[5], but many are warm. If you sleep hot, choose an open-knit design.

Active cooling systems

If habits and bedding are not enough, or if you have night sweats or hot flashes, active systems move heat away from the bed. They differ in how they work:

  • Water-based pads and covers circulate cooled or warmed water through a layer on top of the mattress. The systems on our accessories page include Eight Sleep, which controls each side independently across 55–110°F, and Sleepme, with a range of 55–115°F. They are the most precise option and the most expensive.
  • Air-based systems such as BedJet blow cooled or warmed air under the covers. A dual-zone model lets each side choose, usually for less money than a water system.

Prices, ranges and features change often, so check the current details on our accessories advisor, which compares options against your budget.

Couples with different temperatures

When one partner runs hot and the other runs cold, the solution is to stop sharing a single climate:

  1. Separate covers. Two single duvets or blankets, the norm in much of northern Europe, let each person choose their own weight and fabric. It is the cheapest and often the most effective fix.
  2. Layer by side. The cold partner adds a throw or a heavier blanket; the hot partner uses only a sheet or a light quilt.
  3. Dual-zone bed cooling or heating. Independently controlled water or air systems let each side set its own temperature. This is the best match in our accessories quiz when partners want different temperatures.
  4. Split mattress builds. If you are also choosing a mattress, a split build lets the hot sleeper choose cooler, more breathable materials. Spine Align (Chirobed), our own brand, can build each side of a mattress to a different specification.

Choosing by budget

  • Free: lower the thermostat, run a fan, take a warm shower an hour or two before bed, and switch to separate covers if you share a bed.
  • Under about $200: breathable sheets, a breathable mattress protector and a lighter duvet for the hot sleeper. These solve the problem for many people.
  • Mid-range: a cooling pillow or a mattress with coils or latex next time you replace your mattress.
  • Premium: an active air or water system, ideally dual-zone if you share the bed. Consider it if you have night sweats, hot flashes or a big temperature mismatch with your partner, and the cheaper fixes have not been enough.

When heat may be medical

Night sweats can come from menopause, medications, infections, thyroid problems and other conditions. If they are new, frequent, drenching, or come with fever or weight loss, talk to a doctor rather than just buying a cooling product.
