Practitioner-grade sleep supplements: magnesium, melatonin, glycine and theanine

SomniSmart editorial team · Updated October 27, 2026

Some links on SomniSmart are affiliate links; they never change what we recommend. Spine Align (Chirobed) is our own brand and is always labelled. Affiliate disclosure.

Supplements are the most-asked-about and least-proven part of sleep. This guide summarises what the research actually shows for four popular options, why we only list clinical-grade brands sold through healthcare professionals, and how to use them safely with your provider.

Medical disclaimer: this is general education, not medical advice. Supplements are not approved by the FDA to diagnose, treat, cure or prevent any disease. Talk to a doctor or pharmacist before taking any supplement, especially if you are pregnant or breastfeeding, take medications, have a medical condition, or are giving it to a child or teenager. For chronic insomnia, cognitive behavioural therapy for insomnia (CBT-I) is the recommended first treatment. The products mentioned here are sold through healthcare professionals; ask your provider whether any is right for you.

First: habits beat pills

Before any supplement, check the basics. In a controlled study, 400 mg of caffeine taken even 6 hours before bed reduced total sleep by more than an hour[1]. Alcohol may help you fall asleep but disrupts the second half of the night and reduces REM sleep[2]. A regular wake time, morning daylight and a cool, dark room do more for most people than any capsule.

Why practitioner-grade?

Dietary supplements in the U.S. are lightly regulated, and tested products have sometimes contained very different amounts from their labels. SomniSmart therefore lists only clinical-grade brands that sell mainly through healthcare professionals: Biotics Research, Standard Process, Metagenics, Designs for Health, Xymogen and Pure Encapsulations. These companies manufacture under FDA current Good Manufacturing Practice (cGMP) rules and describe in-house or third-party testing for identity, potency and contaminants. Buying through a practitioner also means someone who knows your health history chooses the product and dose. To be clear, “practitioner-grade” is not a government certification, and it does not make a supplement proven to work. Prices are set by your practitioner or their dispensary, so we show them as “price via practitioner”.

Magnesium

Evidence: limited. Magnesium is an essential mineral and many adults get less than recommended from food[3]. For sleep specifically, NIH's National Center for Complementary and Integrative Health describes the evidence as limited and of low quality[4]. Glycinate (bisglycinate) forms are popular because they are generally gentler on the stomach. The upper limit for supplemental magnesium in adults is 350 mg a day unless a clinician advises otherwise; too much causes diarrhoea and cramping, and magnesium interacts with some antibiotics, bisphosphonates, diuretics and acid-reducing drugs[3].

Practitioner examples: Metagenics Magnesium Glycinate (100 mg magnesium as bis-glycinate per tablet); Standard Process Magnesium Lactate (200 mg per 2 tablets); Standard Process E-Z Mg (70 mg per 6 tablets, from Swiss chard and buckwheat juice powders).

Melatonin (low dose)

Evidence: helpful for jet lag and some body-clock timing problems; not recommended for chronic insomnia. Melatonin signals “night” to your body clock[5]. The American Academy of Sleep Medicine's guideline suggests against using it for chronic insomnia in adults[6]. More is not better; many sleep clinicians start at 0.5–1 mg. The AASM advises talking to a pediatric clinician before giving melatonin to children or teens, as pediatric melatonin overdoses have risen sharply[7][8].

Practitioner examples: Pure Encapsulations Melatonin 0.5 mg (0.5 mg per capsule); Biotics Research Melatonin-B6/Mg (1.5 mg melatonin per half tablet, with vitamin B6 as P-5-P and magnesium glycinate).

Glycine

Evidence: early, small studies. Glycine is an amino acid. Small Japanese studies found that 3 g before bed improved how rested people felt and slightly shortened the time to fall asleep[9]. The trials are few and small. Ask a doctor first if you take clozapine or other antipsychotics.

Practitioner example: Designs for Health Glycine Powder (about 3 g glycine per scoop, the dose used in those studies).

L-theanine

Evidence: mixed, small benefits. L-theanine is an amino acid found in tea. A 2025 systematic review and meta-analysis found small improvements in some sleep measures, with mixed results across studies[10]. It may suit people whose sleep is disturbed by a busy mind. Ask a doctor first if you take blood-pressure medicines or stimulants.

Practitioner example: Xymogen L-Theanine (Suntheanine L-theanine; check the label for the amount per capsule).

Combination formulas

Practitioners also use combination products, such as Metagenics Benesom (melatonin with magnesium, calcium and herbal extracts), Biotics Research Alpha-Theta Ultra PM (L-theanine, herbs, 5-HTP, melatonin and B6), Biotics Research SleepTides (a rice protein hydrolysate) and Standard Process MediHerb Valerian Complex (valerian, passionflower and jujube). Their individual ingredients have mixed evidence, and the combined products have generally not been tested in large trials. Herbal sedatives and 5-HTP can interact with alcohol, sleep medicines and antidepressants, so these need a provider's guidance.

Questions to ask your provider

  • Is there a medical reason for my poor sleep, such as sleep apnoea, restless legs, reflux, pain, mood or a medication side effect, that should be checked first?
  • Which supplement, if any, fits my goal: falling asleep, staying asleep, or shifting my body clock?
  • What dose and timing should I use, and for how long before we review it?
  • Does it interact with any of my medicines or conditions?
  • How do I order it through your practice or dispensary?

How to use supplements safely

  1. Fix caffeine, alcohol and schedule first.
  2. Bring the bottle (or a link) to your doctor or pharmacist and list your other medicines.
  3. Change one thing at a time, at the lowest effective dose, and keep a simple sleep diary for two weeks.
  4. Stop if you notice side effects such as next-day grogginess, headaches or stomach upset.
  5. If insomnia lasts for months, ask about CBT-I and screening for sleep apnoea, restless legs and other causes.
