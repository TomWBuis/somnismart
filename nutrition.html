Sleep Nutrition Guide

What you eat, drink and take, and how it affects sleep.

Plain-language summaries of the evidence from NIH and the American Academy of Sleep Medicine, plus examples of third-party-tested products.

Important: this is education, not medical advice

SomniSmart is not a medical provider. Nothing here is meant to diagnose, treat, cure or prevent any disease, and supplements are not a substitute for treatment of sleep disorders. Talk to your doctor or pharmacist before starting any supplement, especially if you are pregnant or breastfeeding, take any medications, have a medical condition, or are considering anything for a child or teen. Ongoing insomnia, loud snoring, gasping at night or heavy daytime sleepiness deserve a medical evaluation; cognitive behavioural therapy for insomnia (CBT-I) is the recommended first treatment for chronic insomnia.

How we pick products

Supplements are lightly regulated in the U.S., so we prefer products with independent testing: USP Verified (tests that a product contains what the label says and isn’t contaminated) or NSF Certified for Sport (adds screening for banned substances). Start here: timing and habits

Evidence: strongest for everyday changes
  • Caffeine: in a controlled study, 400 mg of caffeine taken even 6 hours before bed reduced total sleep by more than an hour. Try a caffeine cut-off at least 6 hours before bedtime (many people do better with a noon cut-off). Remember tea, cola, energy drinks, pre-workout and dark chocolate.
  • Alcohol: it can make you fall asleep faster, but a review of the research found it disrupts sleep in the second half of the night and reduces REM sleep, with bigger effects at higher doses. Avoid using a “nightcap” as a sleep aid and leave a few hours between your last drink and bed.
  • Meals: avoid large, heavy or spicy meals right before bed, especially if you get reflux; a light snack is fine if hunger keeps you up.
  • Fluids: taper drinks in the last hour or two if you wake to use the bathroom.
  • Consistency beats supplements: a regular wake time, morning daylight and a cool, dark bedroom do more for most people than any pill.

Sources: Drake et al., J Clin Sleep Med 2013 (caffeine 0, 3 or 6 h before bed); Ebrahim et al., Alcohol Clin Exp Res 2013 (alcohol and sleep review)

Magnesium (glycinate / bisglycinate)

Evidence: limited

Magnesium is an essential mineral, and many adults get less than recommended from food. For sleep specifically, NIH’s NCCIH says the evidence is limited and of low quality. Glycinate (bisglycinate) forms are popular because they are generally gentler on the stomach than oxide or citrate.

  • Food first: leafy greens, legumes, nuts, seeds and whole grains.
  • The upper limit for supplemental magnesium in adults is 350 mg/day unless a clinician advises otherwise. Too much can cause diarrhoea and cramping.
  • Ask a doctor first if you have kidney disease, or take antibiotics, bisphosphonates, diuretics or acid-reducing drugs, because magnesium can interact with them.

Melatonin (low dose)

Evidence: helps for jet lag and some timing problems; not recommended for chronic insomnia

Melatonin is a hormone that signals “night” to your body clock. It is most useful for shifting sleep timing (jet lag, delayed sleep phase), not as a nightly sedative. The American Academy of Sleep Medicine’s insomnia guideline suggests against melatonin for chronic insomnia in adults.

  • More is not better: many sleep clinicians start with 0.5–1 mg, taken a few hours or 30–60 minutes before your target bedtime depending on the goal. Ask your doctor what dose and timing fit you.
  • In the U.S. melatonin is sold as a supplement, and tested products have contained very different amounts than their labels state. Choose a third-party-verified product.
  • Children and teens: the AASM advises talking to a pediatric clinician before giving melatonin; melatonin overdoses in children have risen sharply.
  • Ask a doctor first if you are pregnant or breastfeeding, have epilepsy or an autoimmune condition, or take blood thinners, blood-pressure, diabetes or immune-suppressing medicines.

Tart cherry

Evidence: early / small studies

Tart (Montmorency) cherries contain small amounts of melatonin and polyphenols. A small pilot study in 15 older adults with insomnia found modest improvements with tart cherry juice twice daily versus placebo. Promising, but the studies are small. Watch the sugar and calories in juice, and ask a doctor if you have diabetes or take blood thinners.

Glycine

Evidence: early / small studies

Glycine is an amino acid. Small Japanese studies found 3 g before bed improved how rested people felt and slightly shortened the time to fall asleep. The evidence comes from few, small trials. Ask a doctor first if you take clozapine or other antipsychotics.

L-theanine

Evidence: mixed; small benefits

L-theanine is an amino acid found in tea. A 2025 systematic review and meta-analysis found small improvements in some sleep measures, with mixed results across studies. It may suit people whose sleep is disturbed by a busy mind. Ask a doctor first if you take blood-pressure medicines or stimulants.
